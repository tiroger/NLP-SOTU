Shorter sentences, plainer words

From Jefferson (1801) until Wilson revived the spoken address in 1913, the State of the Union was a written report, and the reports got long. Spoken addresses are shorter, and since the arrival of radio and television the reading level has fallen sharply.

Tone and emotion

Each of the ~100,000 sentences is scored by a RoBERTa sentiment model as positive, neutral or negative. A message's net tone is the average of P(positive) − P(negative) across its sentences. For most of the 19th century the average message was close to neutral, sometimes negative. Since the 1940s, with the address aimed at a radio and then television audience, it has been reliably upbeat.

What they talk about

Every paragraph is embedded with a sentence-transformer and classified into one of 19 policy topics by a model trained on 26,000 hand-coded State of the Union sentences plus 750 historical paragraphs labeled by Claude. Native American affairs, currency and territorial expansion fade out in the 20th century; health care, education, immigration and the economy rise.

Where presidents look

Every country a message names, matched to today's borders (Prussia counts as Germany, Persia as Iran, the Soviet Union as Russia). Drag the slider: Britain, Spain and France dominate the early decades, China and the Philippines around 1900, Japan and Germany in the 1940s, the Soviet Union through the Cold War, Iraq in the 2000s, Ukraine and China today.

The words that define them

Distinctive words are found with a log-odds ratio with an informative Dirichlet prior (Monroe, Colaresi & Quinn, 2008). It ranks words a speaker over-uses relative to everyone else, while discounting rare words that would top a raw-frequency list by chance.

A map of 236 years of speeches

Each message becomes a single vector (the average of its paragraph embeddings), and UMAP flattens those vectors onto a page. Speeches that sit close together read alike. The map sorts itself by era almost on its own, because the language changed more over time than it differs between parties.

Era removed subtracts, from each speech, the average of the speeches given within about a decade of it, leaving only how it differs from its contemporaries. Neighbors are then typically 20 years apart instead of 4, and speeches group by president. Party adds almost nothing: among a speech's five closest matches by other presidents, the share from the same party is 52%, no better than chance.

Presidents compared

Each president's average on six measures, shaded by how far it sits from typical. Against all presidents, era dominates: every modern president reads at a low grade level and says “we” often. Against their era (messages within about a decade), what's left is personal style.

          Methods and caveats

          Data

          Texts come from The American Presidency Project at UC Santa Barbara. All of its listed messages are included, among them first-year addresses to joint sessions that are not technically State of the Union addresses. Audience reactions, heckles, editorial notes and signatures are removed before analysis.

          Models

          • Tone: cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-sentiment-latest, sentence level.
          • Emotion: j-hartmann/emotion-english-distilroberta-base, sentence level.
          • Topics: logistic regression on all-mpnet-base-v2 paragraph embeddings, trained on the Policy Agendas Project's hand-coded State of the Union sentences (1946–2025) plus 750 paragraphs from every era labeled by Claude (claude-opus-5-5) under a written rubric.
          • Map: word-weighted mean of paragraph embeddings per speech, projected with UMAP. The era-removed view subtracts a Gaussian-weighted (σ = 10 years) average of neighboring speeches first.
          • Sentences & places: spaCy en_core_web_sm.

          Caveats

          • The models were trained on modern English. Scores for 18th- and 19th-century prose are less reliable, so read the early decades as rough trends.
          • Written messages (most of 1801–1912) were reports to Congress, not speeches to the public, so compare eras with care.
          • Sentiment is not approval. A sentence about a war's victims scores negative even when the president is honoring them.
          • Topic labels are a fixed taxonomy, not discovered clusters. Validated against held-out data, the classifier gets 69% of 1790–1860 paragraphs right, 76% for 1861–1945 and 81% since 1946 (scored against Claude's labels; a version trained on the benchmark alone gets 62% of individual modern sentences right against human coders). Read the heatmap as decade-level trends, not paragraph-level facts. Full numbers: validation report.
