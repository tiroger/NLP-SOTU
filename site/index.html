Shorter sentences, plainer words

From Jefferson (1801) until Wilson revived the spoken address in 1913, the State of the Union was a written report, and the reports got long. Spoken addresses are shorter, and the Flesch–Kincaid reading-level estimate has declined over time. Delivery format, audience and historical language all change together; this comparison does not identify their separate effects.

Tone and emotion

Each of the ~74,000 sentences receives positive, neutral and negative probabilities from a RoBERTa model. A message's net tone averages P(positive) − P(negative). These are model estimates of textual valence, with no independent human validation across historical eras yet. Changes in scores may reflect subject matter, delivery format or model bias as well as rhetoric.

What they talk about

Every paragraph is embedded with a sentence-transformer, with long passages split into complete chunks and pooled by length, then assigned a dominant label from 19 policy topics by a model trained on 26,000 hand-coded State of the Union sentences plus 750 paragraphs from across eras labeled by Claude. These are estimates under a fixed taxonomy; historical labels are weak supervision, not independent human ground truth.

Where presidents look

Detected place names are matched to present-day country proxies: Prussia is displayed as Germany, Persia as Iran, and the Soviet Union as Russia. These historical aliases simplify changing borders; they do not describe territorial continuity. Ambiguous and unresolved names are omitted, and recognition quality can vary by era.

The words that define them

Distinctive words are found with a log-odds ratio with an informative Dirichlet prior (Monroe, Colaresi & Quinn, 2008). It ranks words a speaker over-uses relative to everyone else, while discounting rare words that would top a raw-frequency list by chance.

A map of 236 years of speeches

Each message becomes a word-weighted average of complete paragraph embeddings. UMAP projects these vectors to two dimensions for exploration. Apparent distances and clusters can change with projection settings; nearest-neighbor calculations use the original embedding space.

Era adjusted subtracts a Gaussian-weighted average of other messages near each speech in time (σ = 10 years). This leaves differences from contemporaries, which still mix president, party, topic and delivery format. It does not isolate personal style or a causal party effect.

Party comparisons use presidents as the inference unit. Exact permutations preserve each president’s speeches; a non-significant result does not establish that the parties are equivalent. Computed results and sensitivity checks.

          Methods and caveats

          Data

          Texts come from The American Presidency Project at UC Santa Barbara. All of its listed messages are included, among them first-year addresses to joint sessions that are not technically State of the Union addresses. Audience reactions, heckles, editorial notes and signatures are removed before analysis.

          Models

          • Tone: cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-sentiment-latest, sentence level.
          • Emotion: j-hartmann/emotion-english-distilroberta-base, sentence level.
          • Topics: logistic regression on all-mpnet-base-v2 embeddings of complete paragraphs (long inputs are chunked and pooled), trained on the Policy Agendas Project's hand-coded State of the Union sentences (1946–2025) plus 750 paragraphs from every era labeled by Claude (claude-opus-5-5) under a written rubric.
          • Map: word-weighted mean of paragraph embeddings per speech, projected with UMAP. The era-adjusted view subtracts a Gaussian-weighted (σ = 10 years) average of neighboring speeches first.
          • Sentences & places: spaCy en_core_web_sm.

          Caveats

          • Sentiment and emotion were trained on modern sources, including social media. Independent human validation across eras is pending. Treat these as exploratory model scores, not established changes in presidential happiness or emotion; automated cross-checks do not establish accuracy.
          • Written messages (most of 1801–1912) were reports to Congress, not speeches to the public, so compare eras with care.
          • Sentiment is not approval. A sentence about a war's victims scores negative even when the president is honoring them.
          • Topic labels are a fixed taxonomy. Validation holds out presidents from both benchmark and paragraph training sources. Claude agrees with human benchmark coders on 67.3% overall, and 81% of its high-confidence subset; repeatability is not accuracy. The original paragraph reference excludes texts under 20 words. Independent human historical and short-passage validation is pending. Classification errors can bias decade trends. Full results: validation report.
