Shorter sentences, plainer words

From Jefferson (1801) until Wilson revived the spoken address in 1913, the State of the Union was a written report, and the reports got long. Spoken addresses are shorter, and since the arrival of radio and television the reading level has fallen sharply.

Tone and emotion

Each of the ~100,000 sentences is scored by a RoBERTa sentiment model as positive, neutral or negative. A message's net tone is the average of P(positive) − P(negative) across its sentences. For most of the 19th century the average message was close to neutral, sometimes negative. Since the 1940s, with the address now aimed at a radio and then television audience, it has been reliably upbeat.

What they talk about

Every paragraph is embedded with a sentence-transformer and assigned to the closest of 19 policy topics. Native American affairs, currency and territorial expansion fade out; health care, education, immigration and the economy rise.

The words that define them

Distinctive words are found with a log-odds ratio with an informative Dirichlet prior (Monroe, Colaresi & Quinn, 2008). It ranks words a speaker over-uses relative to everyone else, while discounting rare words that would top a raw-frequency list by chance.

Word trends

Track any word or two-word phrase across 236 years. Separate up to five terms with commas.

A map of 236 years of speeches

Each message becomes a single vector (the average of its paragraph embeddings), and UMAP flattens those vectors onto a page. Speeches that sit close together read alike. The map sorts itself by era almost on its own, because the language changed more over time than it differs between parties.

        Methods and caveats

        Data

        Texts come from The American Presidency Project at UC Santa Barbara. All of its listed messages are included, among them first-year addresses to joint sessions that are not technically State of the Union addresses. Audience reactions, heckles, editorial notes and signatures are removed before analysis.

        Models

        • Tone: cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-sentiment-latest, sentence level.
        • Emotion: j-hartmann/emotion-english-distilroberta-base, sentence level.
        • Topics & map: all-mpnet-base-v2 paragraph embeddings, nearest topic centroid, then UMAP.
        • Sentences & places: spaCy en_core_web_sm.

        Caveats

        • The models were trained on modern English. Scores for 18th- and 19th-century prose are less reliable, so read the early decades as rough trends.
        • Written messages (most of 1801–1912) were reports to Congress, not speeches to the public, so compare eras with care.
        • Sentiment is not approval. A sentence about a war's victims scores negative even when the president is honoring them.
        • Topic labels are a fixed taxonomy, not discovered clusters, and anything that matches no topic well is left as “other.”
